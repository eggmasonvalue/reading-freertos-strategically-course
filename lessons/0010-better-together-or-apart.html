Strategic Programmer / Lesson 10 / ~13 min / Module B

Split by the secret, not by the size

The last question of Module B is the one reviewers get most confidently wrong: should this be one module or two? The naive answer reads line counts. The strategic answer reads coupling — and it will tell you to keep a 9,000-line file whole and to split a 300-line one.

Why this lesson: "This file is too big, split it up" is the single most common cosmetic review comment — and LLMs oblige instantly, carving one cohesive module into three that now share a secret across their new seams. You end up with more files, more interface, and worse coupling. This lesson gives you the real decision rule so you can defend a big module that deserves to stay whole, and demand a split that actually reduces coupling.

The idea: join what shares information, separate what doesn't

Ousterhout Ch. 9: bring two pieces of code together when they share information, are always used together, overlap conceptually, or are hard to understand in isolation; keep them apart when the boundary between them can be narrow.1 The deciding variable is never length — it's how much has to cross the line. A boundary is only worth drawing where the traffic across it is thin. Draw one through the middle of a shared secret and you haven't made two modules; you've made one module with a wound running through it.

What this does not claim. It is not "big files are good" or "never split" — it's that coupling, not line count, decides the boundary. A large module can be perfectly correct (tasks.c) and still have real, unrelated costs — merge contention, slower navigation, longer compiles — which the rule doesn't deny; it just says those costs are the wrong reason to cut through a secret. And the rule cuts both ways: it demands you split when a genuinely narrow seam is hiding inside a big file. The claim is about where the line goes (along thin seams, never through shared state), not about how many files you end up with.

apart when

The two sides share almost nothing. They meet through a handful of functions with a stable, published signature. The secret each keeps stays on its own side. A narrow line — worth drawing.

apart wrongly

You split by size or "cleanliness". Now a shared data structure straddles the seam; both sides know its layout; every change touches both. A wide, leaky line — the split made things worse.

Specimen: one enormous file, and one deliberately separate one

The kernel's own layout is the argument. Look at the two biggest source files:

queue.c    3391 lines   // queues/semaphores/mutexes: the ring buffer + blocking

Why tasks.c is correctly monolithic

The scheduler, the task-state machine, the ready and delayed lists, and the TCB_t structure itself are one tightly-woven secret. The context switcher reads the ready lists; the delay logic edits the same lists; every path mutates TCB fields directly. Crucially, the definition of TCB_t lives inside tasks.c and appears in no public header — outside the file a task is only an opaque handle:

/* task.h — the outside world gets a handle, never the struct */
typedef struct tskTaskControlBlock * TaskHandle_t;

./reference/FreeRTOS-Kernel/include/task.h — lines 98–100

./reference/FreeRTOS-Kernel/tasks.c — lines 377–459 (the full TCB_t definition)

Splitting "the scheduler" from "task creation" from "the delayed-list logic" into separate files would force TCB_t's layout to become shared across those seams — the secret would leak out of exactly the one place that's allowed to know it. The pieces are hard to understand apart and they share information constantly: by Ch. 9, they belong together. The size is a consequence of correct grouping, not a defect.

Why queue.c is correctly separate

A queue keeps a completely different secret — a ring buffer, its head/tail pointers, its item copies. It needs tasks for only one thing: to block a caller when the queue is full/empty and wake it later. And it gets that through a deliberately narrow published interface — a handful of functions, and it never once touches a TCB's fields:

/* the entire vocabulary queue.c uses to speak to tasks.c */
vTaskPlaceOnEventList( ... );        // "park this caller until I signal"
xTaskRemoveFromEventList( ... );     // "wake the highest-priority waiter"
vTaskMissedYield();  xTaskCheckForTimeOut( ... );  xTaskGetSchedulerState();

./reference/FreeRTOS-Kernel/queue.c — exact ...Task... call sites at lines 336, 774, 833, 965, 1121–1126, 1682, 1723, 1779–1806, 1902, 2413, 2525–2598, 3164, 3363

Verdict Same codebase, opposite decisions, one rule. tasks.c stays whole because its parts share the TCB_t secret and reading them apart would be harder and would leak that secret. queue.c stands apart because it shares almost nothing with tasks — the boundary between them is a thin, published set of ~8 functions across which no structure layout ever travels. The decision was made on width of the seam, and line count fell out afterward. That thin line is also, in connascence terms, the whole point: a narrow interface keeps the strong connascence inside each file and only weak connascence crossing between them.

The honest borderline: bigness is a prompt, not a verdict

None of this makes "large file" harmless. A big module is a legitimate prompt to check whether a narrow seam is hiding inside it — sometimes one genuinely is, and Ch. 9 says split it out (that's exactly how timers.c, event_groups.c, and stream_buffer.c each earned their own file: each keeps its own secret and talks to the kernel through a thin interface). The error is treating size as the verdict instead of the prompt. You still have to look for the thin line before you cut. If the only line available runs through a shared structure, there is no good split — and the right move is to leave it whole and make it readable inside instead (naming, comments, internal helpers — Module D).

Deep dive — why the costs of a big file are bounded but the cost of a bad cut isn't

The steelman below is right that a 9,000-line file has real costs — so why does coupling still win the tie? Because the two kinds of cost scale differently. The costs of bigness — merge conflicts, scroll distance, compile time, "where is this defined" — are real but bounded and local: they annoy the people editing that one file, and every one of them has a cheap mitigation that doesn't move the seam. You can carve tasks.c into clearly-named static helpers and #if-fenced sections (it is), split the editing experience with folding and good names (Module D), and speed compiles with other tools. You get modularity within the file without exporting the secret. The cost of a bad cut — splitting through TCB_t — is different in kind: it publishes the layout to a second module, so now every future change to that structure touches both files, forever, and the coupling grows with each new field and each new caller (it's A4 leakage with a file boundary bolted on). Bounded-and-fixable versus unbounded-and-compounding: that asymmetry is why you tolerate a big cohesive file and refuse a tidy-looking leaky split. "Big" is an ergonomics problem with local fixes; "leaked" is a design problem that metastasises.

The strongest case for splitting anyway (steelman)

"Split big files" isn't just laziness — it has serious backing. State it at strength, then synthesise.

The case against the big file
  1. Single Responsibility Principle. "Scheduler + task state + lists + creation + delays" is manifestly more than one responsibility. A 9,000-line file violates SRP on its face — that's several reasons to change living in one place.
  2. Team-scale friction. Big files are merge-conflict magnets, slow to navigate, intimidating to newcomers, and serialise work — two people can't comfortably edit different parts without stepping on each other.
  3. Testability. Smaller units with explicit interfaces are easier to test in isolation; a monolith forces you to test everything through the top-level API, which is coarser and slower.

The synthesis. Every point is real, and every one is about something other than coupling — which is exactly why they don't overturn the rule, they qualify it. SRP (1) is right that tasks.c has many functions, but "single responsibility" at the module level is "one secret, one reason the representation changes," and by that reading the scheduler, state machine, and lists are one responsibility: they all exist to safely mutate TCB_t state. Splitting them gives you files that each have fewer lines but share a reason to change — worse SRP, not better. Points (2) and (3) are genuine costs, and the honest answer is that they're paid down by internal structure and tooling, not by cutting through the secret: name the sections, use static helpers, fold the file, test through the published ...Task... seam that already exists. When a sub-part does have its own secret and a thin interface, Ch. 9 agrees — split it (that's timers.c). So the position isn't "never split big files" — it's "let the width of the seam decide, and solve the ergonomics of bigness with Module-D readability, not by leaking a structure across a new boundary."

Munger inversion — where the rule dies Invert it: the worst split is the one that looks tidy — two neat files — while a shared secret quietly straddles the seam. Now every change touches both sides, the layout is known in two places (leakage), and you cross the boundary constantly. You didn't reduce complexity; you added a border and left the coupling. A split that increases what crosses the line is strictly worse than the big file it replaced. Ask not "is this file big?" but "if I cut here, what has to travel across the cut?"
Red flag to name in reviews A split (or a merge) justified by size, tidiness, or "one class per file" rather than by coupling. The words: "What crosses this proposed boundary? If a shared data structure or invariant does, the split just spreads one secret across two modules — keep them together. If only a few stable calls cross, the split is real; make that interface the published one." Push the model to name the seam's width, never its line count.
